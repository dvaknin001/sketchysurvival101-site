$5 of Foil Behind Your Radiator Cuts Its Wall Heat Loss in Half

Every radiator on an outside wall is heating the street. One flat sheet of kitchen foil, facing the metal across the air gap, sends that heat back into the room.

The fix in one line

Buy a 50 square foot roll of heavy duty aluminum foil. Tape a sheet flat to the wall behind each radiator on an outside wall, facing the radiator across the air gap. In a university test, one plain sheet of kitchen foil cut the heat leaking through the wall behind the radiator by more than half.

The feel test: hand on the wall just above a running radiator, then 3 feet to the side.

The patch by the radiator is warm, sometimes hot. The wall beside it is room temperature. Same plaster, same studs, same cold night outside.

Foil on the wall, facing the radiator. The infrared bounces back toward the radiator and up into the room.

Most of that heat comes back into the room. A slice soaks through the plaster and framing and leaks out through the siding. How big a slice depends on what is inside the wall. See the flashlight test on page 3.

Why foil works

Emissivity is how strongly a surface gives off infrared, on a scale from 0 to 1. A painted wall sits near 0.9. Bright aluminum foil sits near 0.03. A surface that barely gives off infrared also barely absorbs it, so foil reflects roughly 97 percent of the infrared that hits it. The plaster behind it runs cooler, and a cooler inside face drives less heat out through the wall. Same reason a vacuum flask is silvered inside.

The foil must face open air

Infrared has to cross empty space to be reflected. Foil pressed between two solid layers just passes heat through by contact, so foil board glued face down onto plaster does almost nothing. Your radiator already gives you the air space: panel radiators hang an inch or two off the plaster, and old cast iron radiators on legs usually sit 2 to 4 inches out.

Shopping list

  • Heavy duty aluminum foil, the 50 square foot roll. At Walmart it is 4 dollars and 98 cents. Heavy duty, not the thin stuff: crinkles barely hurt the reflection, tears do.
  • Aluminum foil tape, the kind sold in the heating aisle for sealing ductwork. Rated for heat, reflective itself, about 10 dollars a roll.
  • Painter's tape instead, if you rent and need the wall to come clean later. Fine on a hot water radiator wall. Foil tape holds longer.
  • Thin cardboard, for radiators too close to the wall to get your hands behind.
  • Tape measure, scissors, a rag, and a vacuum or duster wand.

Which radiators

  • Only radiators on outside walls. An interior wall radiator warms a wall that is inside your house anyway. Most radiators sit under windows on outside walls, so start with those.
  • Skip hot water baseboard, the long low metal strips along the floor. Baseboard already has a steel back plate and heats almost entirely with moving air.
  • Skip every electric heater, including electric baseboard. See the safety box.
Flat to the wall, edges taped, pressed down hard with a rag.

Step by step

  1. Turn the heat off and let the radiator cool until you can rest your hand on it.
  2. Vacuum the gap behind it first. The dust back there now ends up on your foil.
  3. Measure the back face of the radiator. Cut the foil an inch bigger than that on every side.
  4. The 5 dollar roll is 12 inches wide, so check the width on your box. For a radiator 2 feet tall, run 3 strips sideways, each above the last, overlapping by an inch. That covers about 34 inches of height.
  5. For a radiator 4 feet wide, each strip is about 50 inches long.
  6. Face the shiny side toward the radiator out of habit. The difference between the sides is tiny, and both reflect well.
  7. Run foil tape along all four edges and press it down hard with a rag.
  8. Too tight to reach behind? Tape the foil to thin cardboard, foil side out. Slide the panel down from the top, cardboard against the wall, foil facing the metal. Do not let it touch the radiator.
  9. Panel radiators on wall brackets: cut a slot in the cardboard for each bracket before you slide it in.
  10. Radiator inside a wooden or metal cover: lift the cover off, do the wall the same way, put the cover back.
  11. When the heat comes back on, feel the wall just above the foil again. It will be noticeably cooler.

Keep every edge clear

Do not wrap foil around the pipes or the valve. Do not cover the top of the radiator, and do not let foil hang down and block the gap at the floor. A radiator pulls cool air in at the bottom and pushes warm air out the top. Choke that path, and you lose more heat than the foil saves.

Once a year

Run a duster wand or a vacuum brush down the gap. Dust absorbs far more infrared than clean foil. Tarnish matters less: bare aluminum creeps from about 3 percent emissivity to about 8 to 12 percent, then levels off, and still reflects close to 90 percent.

The two places this goes wrong

  • Electric heaters, including electric baseboard. The maker sets the clearances around the heating elements, and foil conducts electricity. Nothing metal goes near the wiring or the elements. Leave electric heaters exactly the way they came.
  • Foam behind a steam radiator. Steam radiators run at about 215 F at the surface. Foil is fine there, since aluminum does not melt until around 1,200 F. Foam board is not: polystyrene starts to soften around 200 F and will sag and give off fumes against hot metal. On steam, use foil and foil tape on the wall, and nothing else.

The flashlight test

  1. Pick an outlet on an outside wall and switch off its breaker.
  2. Pull the cover plate and shine a flashlight into the crack around the box.
  3. Fiber or foam: the wall is insulated, and the foil will do less.
  4. Bare wood and a dark gap: nothing in there, and the foil will pay.
  5. Brick or block: solid masonry with no insulation at all. That is where foil helps most.
  6. Put the cover back and the breaker on before you do anything else.
Breaker off, cover plate off, light into the crack around the box.

About 9 million American homes heat with a hot water or steam boiler, most built decades ago. A Pacific Northwest National Laboratory study found up to 43 percent of homes built before 1992 have little or no wall insulation. Britain's Building Research Establishment found the gain is minimal on a wall with filled cavities.

At about 17 dollars, the job pays for itself the first winter on an oil heated house, and usually within a winter or two on gas.

The expensive fix

Blowing loose insulation into the walls runs about 1 to 2 dollars a square foot installed, somewhere between 2,000 and 8,000 dollars for a whole house. It fixes the entire wall and is the right long term answer. The foil is what you can do this weekend.
