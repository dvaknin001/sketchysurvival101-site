The 12 Dollar Window Fix vs Storm Windows: Every Number

A shrink film kit traps a still layer of air over a drafty single pane window. Installed storm windows use the same trick for a lot more money. Here is every figure from the video, the install steps, the safety checks, and the pipe checklist.

What it is

A window shrink film kit is a big sheet of thin clear plastic and a roll of double sided tape. You tape the frame, press the film on, and warm it with a hair dryer until it pulls tight. That leaves a pocket of still air between the film and the glass, usually half an inch to a couple of inches deep, depending on the frame.

The physics in one line: almost none of a single pane window's insulation comes from the glass. It comes from thin layers of air at the glass surface. Wind speeds heat loss on the outside. A sealed, still air gap slows it down. The plastic is the fence that keeps that air from flowing.

The result: on a drafty single pane window, the film can cut heat loss through the glass by about half. That is the same range storm windows promise.

Storm windows vs film, honestly

Film winsPrice. Speed: a freeze this weekend, film tonight. Same still air gap trick over single glass.
Storm windows winLast for decades. Tougher. Let you open the sash in summer. Coated (low e) versions bounce more heat back inside.

Staying in the house for 20 years? Storm windows are a solid upgrade. Film is a one winter measure you redo each fall.

The cold waterfall

Air touching freezing glass cools, gets heavier, and slides down the pane. It spills off the sill and rolls across the floor, and warm room air gets pulled in at the top to replace it. That is why a locked window still feels drafty: some of the chill is outside air sneaking around the sash, and a lot of it is your own room air falling off cold glass.

With film on, the inside face stays far warmer than the glass behind it, so less air sinks. When the tape seal runs unbroken around the whole frame, it also blocks air leaking in around the sash.

The key idea

A storm window's second pane is not what earns its number. The gap does. Still air is one of the best cheap insulators there is, and moving air is one of the worst. Film builds the same gap from plastic instead of glass and aluminum.

Shopping list

  • Shrink film kit with double sided tape (about 9 to 20 dollars for about five windows)
  • A hair dryer, not a heat gun
  • Scissors or a utility knife for trimming
  • A lead test kit if your house was built before 1978
  • Optional: caulk for gaps between the window frame and the wall

Hang the film

  1. Find your worst windows. On a cold evening, move the back of your hand slowly along the glass and around the edges of the sash. Moving air goes to the top of the list. Any window above a sink or near plumbing goes first.
  2. Check the paint. Built before 1978? Read the lead box on the right before any tape touches the frame.
  3. Clean the frame where the tape goes and let it dry completely. Work while the room is warm. Double sided tape grips badly on cold or damp surfaces.
  4. Tape the frame all the way around, with no gaps.
  5. Cut the film larger than the frame, following your kit's directions for the extra margin.
  6. Press it on starting at the top, then down the sides, then along the bottom. Keep it smooth, but don't fuss over wrinkles yet.
  7. Dry the sill, then plug in the hair dryer. Set it on high and hold it about 6 inches from the film, or the distance your kit lists. Keep it moving.
  8. Stop once the film is tight. Shrinking too hard pulls the tape loose at the corners. Never let the nozzle touch the film.
  9. Trim the extra and put the scraps straight into the outside trash. Unplug the dryer and keep the cord clear of water.
  10. Check the seal. Run your hand around the edge again. No moving air means the pocket is sealed.

Lead paint: the tape mistake

  • If your house was built before 1978, the paint on the window frames may contain lead. Windows are one of the worst spots, because opening and closing them grinds old paint into dust.
  • Double sided tape can rip that paint off when you pull it down in spring.
  • Never stick tape onto paint that's chipped or flaking.
  • A hardware store lead test kit gives you a first answer, not the final word. The EPA points to a certified inspector, or treating old paint as if it contains lead.
  • In spring, warm the tape with the hair dryer first, then peel it slowly back on itself.
  • Never scrape or sand old window paint to clean up what's left.

Fire, escape, and air

  • Hair dryer, never a heat gun. A heat gun can run hundreds of degrees hotter. It can melt the film fast, and heat near the edge of cold glass can damage the window.
  • Plastic film burns. Keep space heaters at least 3 feet from a covered window, the same as a curtain. Keep candles off the sill and away from the film.
  • Bedroom windows are fire escapes. Everyone in the room must be able to open them fast, without tools. Never screw anything solid over one or block it with furniture.
  • Kerosene or unvented gas heater? Leave one window uncovered and cracked. Those heaters need outside air, and a sealed room is where carbon monoxide builds. Keep a working carbon monoxide alarm.
  • Plastic sheeting can suffocate a small child. Dry cleaning bags carry the same warning. Scraps go straight to the outside trash.

Watch for water

Warm indoor air carries moisture from cooking, showers, and your own breath. When it touches a cold enough surface, water beads up. A good seal helps, because the surface facing the room stays warmer. A leaky seal lets damp air slip behind the plastic onto freezing glass, where it can't dry out, and mold can start.

  • Check every covered window often, especially in the first weeks.
  • Water running down the glass or pooling on the sill: open it up, dry it, check the seal, and lower indoor humidity. If it keeps coming back, look for other leaks.
  • Run the bathroom fan during showers and the range hood while you cook.
  • The EPA suggests indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent.

Two other options

Bubble wrap, for windows nobody looks through

Mist the glass with plain water from a spray bottle. Press the bubble side flat against the wet glass. It can cling for the season, though some pieces may need tape. The window still opens, so it suits a basement or bathroom. Wipe the sill dry after.

A reusable interior storm panel

A simple frame of thin wood strips, cut to fit snug inside the window opening, with shrink film stretched over both faces and foam weatherstrip around the edge so it presses in tight. It comes out and goes back in each fall. On a bedroom window, make sure anyone can pull it out fast.

What film can't do

  • It does nothing for air leaking between the window frame and the wall behind the trim. A bead of caulk handles that.
  • It won't fix rotten wood or cracked glass.
  • It can't reliably protect a pipe inside a cold outside wall. The pipe needs its own defense.

Pipe checklist

In a lot of kitchens, the sink sits under a window on an outside wall, with the water lines in that wall or the cabinet below. Close the cabinet on a frigid night and the pipes sit in a dark box against a cold wall.

  • Seal the holes. Find where pipes and wires pass through outside walls and seal them with caulk or a can of foam. The Illinois research pointed at holes like these, where cable and phone lines enter. Leave vents and combustion air openings clear.
  • Sleeve the pipes. Slip foam pipe sleeves over any pipe you can reach in a cabinet, a crawlspace, or the garage. A 6 foot length usually costs a few dollars.
  • Open the cabinets on nights below about 20 F so room heat reaches the pipes. Move cleaners out of reach of kids first.
  • Let it drip. Let the faucet on an outside wall drip a little cold water. It helps, but a drip is no guarantee.
  • Hold the heat steady day and night, and never below 55 F, even when you're away. That is the Red Cross advice. A deep setback at night is when a cold house gets riskier.
  • Find your main water shutoff before the freeze, and make sure it actually turns.

If a pipe freezes

  • Thaw it slowly with a hair dryer or warm towels.
  • Never use a torch or open flame.
  • If a pipe lets go, shut the main water valve right away. Every minute of spraying water adds to the bill.
  • Carbon monoxide alarm sounding, or a fire: get everyone out and call nine one one.
