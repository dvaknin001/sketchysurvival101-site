The 10 Dollar Box That Finishes Cooking With No Fuel

Boil for 10 minutes, seal the pot in a box of blankets, and the stored heat keeps cooking for hours. The physics, the build, the boil times, and the safety line that keeps the food from making you sick.

What it is

A storage tote or cardboard carton stuffed with the blankets in your closet. You boil the pot on the stove for 10 minutes, seal it in the box, and walk away. Hours later the food is still above 170 degrees and still cooking, on no extra fuel. It is the old hay box cooker, rebuilt for 10 dollars.

The trick: a boiling pot stops getting hotter at 212 degrees. After that, the flame is only replacing heat that leaks out the sides. Insulate the pot and the leak slows to a crawl, so the stored heat does the long simmer for you.

Where the fuel actually goes

10 minTime a flame spends raising the pot to a boil. After that the water holds at 212 degrees, no matter how big the flame.
about 65 WHeat a lidded pan of 4 liters of boiling water leaks on a bench, from a 2014 university lab test in Bangladesh. It fell to 149 degrees in about two and a half hours.
about 3,000 WA camp stove burner wide open. Even turned low it puts out many times what the pot leaks, so the extra leaves as steam.

What the box does to the leak

about 18 WSame 4 liters, same lab, now inside a padded Wonderbag cooking bag from South Africa. It took about 9 hours to fall to 149 degrees. Insulation cut the leak to roughly a quarter.
172°F at 4 hrA 1919 United States Department of Agriculture bulletin packed a 6 quart kettle of boiling water in a box of crumpled newspaper. Still 172 degrees after 4 hours.
155°F at 8 hrSame 1919 test, 8 hours in. That full kettle of newspaper insulation is where the 170 degree promise comes from.

Think of the pot as a battery

The stove charges it in 10 minutes. The box only has to stop it draining.

about 500 WhRough heat a 6 quart pot at a boil holds above room temperature.
65 vs 18 WLeaking at 65 watts, most of it is gone by bedtime. Leaking at 18, it lasts through the night.

Why half raw is not the risk

Cooking is chemistry that runs on temperature, and most of it runs well below a boil.

about 150°FRice starch starts to swell and soften.
about 160°FTough collagen in a cheap cut of beef starts turning to gelatin. That change needs time more than heat, which is why stew takes hours on any stove.
212 to 172°FA pot drifting down over 4 hours stays above both those numbers the whole time.
22 to 48%Energy the 2014 Bangladesh team saved on foods needing short simmers. Rice, potatoes, chicken, and mixed vegetables all came out done, and they rated the texture better than the stovetop. On beans and long stews the saving should be larger, but that study did not test them.

Build the box

  1. Pick a shell with walls and a lid that leaves 4 to 6 inches of space around the pot on every side, and a few inches above it.
  2. Pack the bottom 4 inches solid with soft dry insulation. Press it firm, do not crush it, since the trapped air is what insulates.
  3. Line the sides so the pot sits in a nest. Keep the packing firm, the top layer loose.
  4. Set a wood cutting board or a folded towel in the bottom of the nest. A pot straight off the boil is 212 degrees and never sits on bare plastic or foam.
  5. Get a real rolling boil on the stove, then move the pot straight into the nest with the lid on tight.
  6. Press a folded towel or pillow over the pot lid, since heat rises and the top is where most of it leaves. Then close the box lid.

Get the pot right

  • Use a tight lid. A loose lid dumps steam and heat.
  • Keep the pot at least three quarters full. The Bangladesh team advised 80 percent full, since their small loads cooled fastest. The heat is stored in the food, so a half empty pot is a half charged battery.
  • For a small batch, set the small pot inside a bigger pot of boiling water and box the pair, so the water carries the heat. That fix is from the 1919 bulletin.

Boil first, then box it

Get a full rolling boil on the stove, move the pot into the nest, close it up, and leave it alone. The rice, oatmeal, and bean times come straight from the 1919 government bulletin.

Kidney beans carry a toxin, do this first

Red kidney beans hold a natural toxin that a low slow heat does not destroy. Slow cookers have caused outbreaks because they never get hot enough.

4 or 5 beansAs few as this, eaten raw, can set off hours of vomiting, per the Food and Drug Administration.
  • Soak the beans at least 5 hours, then pour that water off.
  • Boil them in fresh water for a full 30 minutes. Both steps are the agency's own advice.
  • Only then give them about 6 hours in the box, the time the 1919 bulletin used.

The box handles anything you would boil or stew. It will not fry, roast, bake, or brown.

The rules that keep it safe

Raw meat gets fully cooked at the boil, before it ever goes in the box. After that, one number keeps it safe, and it is 140 degrees.

40 to 140°FThe Department of Agriculture danger zone, where bacteria can double in as little as 20 minutes.
over 2 hrTime in that zone after which food gets thrown out.
140°FAt or above, and cooked through, it is safe to serve. Below 140, you do not know how long it sat, so throw it out. Reheating will not make it safe.
  • Keep the pot full and the lid shut. Every peek dumps heat you cannot get back, and small loads cool much faster than full ones.
  • For small children, anyone pregnant, older people, or anyone with a weak immune system, bring it back to a full boil right before serving, even at 140 or above.
  • Never put a hot brick, heated stone, or any heat source inside a cardboard or plastic box. The 1919 bulletin warns a hot stone can set paper packing on fire. The box holds a pot of hot food and nothing else.
  • Keep the box on the floor of the warmest room, on a rug, away from where a child can tip it. It is 6 quarts of near boiling liquid in a soft package.

Carbon monoxide: the stove stays outside

The box makes no exhaust, so the long cook can happen inside. The flame that starts the boil cannot.

400+ a yearAmericans killed by accidental carbon monoxide, not counting fires, per the Centers for Disease Control. Camp stoves, grills, and generators are on its list. Never run them inside a home, basement, or garage, even with a door open.

Cold kitchens and overnight pots

Heat leaks in proportion to the gap between the pot and the room, so a cold kitchen drains the pot faster.

130° gapA 200 degree pot in a 70 degree kitchen.
155° gapThe same pot in a 45 degree kitchen. It leaks about a fifth faster, by simple proportion.

The fix is more blanket and a fuller pot, in the warmest room, on a rug, never on bare concrete. For the overnight oats, boil 10 minutes at 10 at night. Beans still get their full 30 minute boil first. In the morning, the thermometer goes in before anything else. The 8 hour test reading was 155 with a full kettle, so a well packed full pot should stay above 140. Under 140, throw it out.

The fuel math

about 2 hrWhat one 16 ounce canister runs a single Coleman burner on high. The spec is both burners for about an hour, so call it 4 to 6 hours at a simmer. That part is an estimate, since Coleman does not publish low flame figures.
3 potsBeef stew cooked the normal way, about two thirds of a canister each, gets roughly three pots from two canisters.
6 to 8 potsWith the box you only boil, about a quarter to a third of a canister each. Two canisters make six to eight pots, roughly double. Treat these as estimates.
about $5.50A canister at Walmart on a normal day. The day a storm gets a name, that shelf is one of the first to empty. A box stretches every canister you already stored.

Keep canisters outdoors in a shaded, airy spot, never in the house or a closed garage.
